4. Layout & spacing

The rule underneath every section below this one: a primitive carries no outer margin. A button, a field, a tile, a hero, a speech bubble — every one of them stops exactly where its own border stops. Nothing on this site decides how much air goes above or below it. That is the caller's job, and .stack is how the caller does it.

Which is what makes a component portable: moving one from one page to another never brings its spacing with it, and a page that wants things closer together changes one class instead of fighting a margin it did not write.

No stack — what "no outer margin" looks like

Two real fields, as direct children of a plain block. Nothing is broken here: there is simply no air, because neither field claims any. This is the default state of every primitive on this page.

.stack — 1rem

A flex column with a gap, and the default answer. Every form on this site is one, and so is most of what sits under a hero.

.stack--tight — 0.4rem

For things that belong to each other rather than a sequence of separate asks — the camera form, and the list of hints on a game page.

Two gaps, and this section is the first time either has been shown. Both shipped with the rule itself, in the commit that adopted this kit, and /kit went straight from borders to the score header — so the one rule every component below depends on lived in a CSS comment and nowhere a page author would meet it (#59).

Finding that out cost .field its margin-bottom, which shipped in the same commit as the rule it broke. A <label> is inline, so in ordinary flow the margin never applied at all — which is why the fields in the first box above are touching, and why they were touching in §10 before this section existed. Inside a .stack the label becomes a flex item, the margin applies, and it adds to the gap: every form on this site measured 2rem, and .stack--tight measured 1.4rem. The three boxes above only became honest demonstrations when it came out.

There was a third step, .stack--loose at 1.8rem. It shipped with the other two and no page ever used it, so it is gone rather than given a use invented to keep it (#58's rule, taking its second branch for the first time). Putting a step back is one line — and it arrives with a demo here, because new design is drawn on this page first.

9. Buttons

Three tiers, and two specials. Everything is thumb-sized because everyone is holding a drink, and nothing is full width — a button is snug to its words, and a row that wants to fill says so at the row.

Primary — the CTA. The press that advances. One per screen.

Secondary — the other direction off this same screen: a wizard's back, the other answer to a destructive question, a dialog's No?. Agrees with the primary on font so the two read as one pair; differs on fill, which is the entire hierarchy.

Tertiary — ad-hoc. Reloads this screen with something different, or leaves it sideways. Never the alternative to a primary, which is what separates deal us another (stays put) from undo that (goes back).

The two specials, which are not a tier: the hint is the only yellow control on the site and is deliberately loud, and What? is a dialog's aside — neither answer to the question being asked.

Disabled, which any tier can be.

Settled in #107, which found one class saying close, actually, no, keep them and back to the controls — six words and four unrelated jobs on one look. The visible cost was on the door: on my own and actually, no came out identical while one went forward and one threw away your typing. Same ticket took text-transform: uppercase off the primary, so buttons now say what was written rather than shouting it.

Grouping — snug is a property of the group, not the button

A lone button is snug to its own words and needs no container — that is the whole rule for it. Two or more buttons that belong together are a group, and the group says which of four shapes it is. Nothing below is full width; a group that wants the whole column says so here, at the row.

.btnrow — side by side, packed to the start

The default. Wraps when it runs out of room, which on a 390px phone it will.

.btnrow--split — one at each end

For two buttons that are not a pair. The team dashboard is the only caller, and the only button group a guest ever meets: the rules, and a joke that changes nothing. Packed together they would read as a choice between two related things.

.btnrow--end — packed to the end

A confirm and the way out of it, quietest first so it reads left to right as the stakes rise and the primary lands under your thumb. This is the shape a dialog (§11) and a door screen already use — .modal__actions and .door__actions are callers of this class now, not private rows of their own. The three full-page confirmations use it too (/admin/reset, /admin/end, /admin/delete-team): a whole page asking are you sure is the same act as a box asking it, so it is laid out the same way.

.btncol — stacked, matched to the widest

For peers too wide to share a row. Snug left them as a two-step staircase — the shape the bottom of /admin/controls had. The group is still snug: the column is only as wide as the longer label, and neither button goes full bleed.

Settled in #106. .btnrow had lived in the stylesheet since this kit was built with zero callers on the site — the kit demoing a shape no page had, which is §0's trap running backwards. It turned out not to be unused so much as re-written three times: the dialog, the door and the judging row were each a private flex row with the same gap. Two of them are callers now. The judging row (admin only) stays its own thing, because it is the one row here that deliberately fills — two verdict buttons splitting a gallery card — which is the opposite of snug.

10. Form fields

Forms POST and redirect. No client-side validation theatre.

field() from src/render.js, the same call onboarding makes for all nine of its fields. The label wraps the control instead of pointing at it with for — there is no id to keep in sync, which is the one way this markup can break silently, and it breaks by labelling the wrong box.

The last one is a select, which Guess Who uses to name a person — it carries a member id and shows a name, so nobody loses a point because their partner typed their pet name at the door. It has to draw its own arrow, because .input sets -webkit-appearance: none and takes the native one away.

The three controls — sunk, raised, and which one a select is

A box you type in, a box you pick from and a box you press are three different objects, and here they are together, which is the only way to tell. Until #104 they were one shell wearing three fills: the same --edge-2 border, the same square corners, the same --tap, and a hard shadow whose only difference was that a text box's fell inside at 12% black — invisible on a page whose background is a gradient. /shots was the proof: two selects and a button, all paper, three white slabs in a column.

One rule with two directions, and it needs no words: sunk means you fill it, raised means you press it. The text box carries --c-gray at --edge-2 inside its own edge, so the paper reads as cut. The button drops --shadow-1 outside itself onto the page, so it reads as sitting on top. Grey rather than black for the inside one: at 4px black reads as a second border and the box gains a weight it should not have next to a primary.

A select is a button. That is the decision #104 was asked for, and it is why the middle control above is snug, raised, and goes down under a thumb like everything else you press. It keeps .input for the border, the tap target and the serif, then undoes the one thing that class asserts. Two other treatments were drawn at phone width first: a page-wide sunk box with a better arrow still read as somewhere to type, and a snug box that stayed sunk went inert beside a raised look and a raised submit — the only control on the page that did nothing when you touched it.

The arrow is a slab of ink down the right edge with the triangle knocked out in paper, not a 12px triangle floating in white. Still gradients rather than an image — no asset to ship, and no blur anywhere ever (#5). Only the value stays serif: a select shows content where a button shows a word the site chose, and setting a guest's answer in Archivo Black would claim it was ours.

A select sizes itself to its widest option, which is the one thing that makes snug dangerous: what it answers on /shots holds prompts, a prompt is a sentence, and the page came out 454px wide on a 390px phone. The cap is calc(100vw - 3rem) and it is deliberately not max-width: 100% — a percentage is ignored while the browser works out how wide the thing wants to be, so the page grows anyway and only the select ends up bounded. scripts/screenshot.js' overflow check is what caught it.

11. Modal

Fires on every hint reveal, saying a different thing the first time a team ever asks (free, a gift) than every time after (the price, a fine). Built by hintModal() in src/render.js and rendered already open by the server, because the hint has been revealed and charged by the time it appears — it announces, it never asks. On a real page it sits outside .app — because anim-page animates a transform and a transformed ancestor would pin a position: fixed box to the page instead of the viewport — and first, ahead of it, so the box you are looking at is also the first thing read and the first thing tabbed to. That is the whole of this site's focus management (#31, ADR-document-order-instead-of-focus-management): no trap, no move-in, no restore, no script. Here it is at the end of the page instead, because this is the one place that opens it by hand.

Shown here in its paid framing. The free one is the same box with different words, and there is only one #hint-modal on a page — demoing both would mean a second copy, which is the thing this page no longer keeps.

The two words — No? and Okay?

A modal has exactly two answers and they are always the same two words: No? and Okay?. Not fine, not lovely, not no thanks — this box had already invented three words for two slots before anyone else got a turn. The question marks are the register (#37): the site sounds unsure of its own offer while it takes your points. They hold on admin surfaces too. The admin is Dieter, who knows the voice, so there is no quieter register for HQ.

The order is as fixed as the words: aside, then deny, then confirm, left to right — and the confirm stays rightmost on a destructive press too. Flipping it there would move the only irreversible button on the site to the place the muscle memory says is safe.

The deny is optional. Plenty of modals will not have one: the hint box above does not, because the hint is already revealed and charged by the time it appears — there is nothing left to say no to. A box that only announces gets one button. What is not optional is the words, which is why they live in modalActions() in src/render.js and not in each caller.

The third slot is neither answer. The hint box's What? is a link to the rules, not a refusal, so it keeps its own words and sits outside the pair. Two buttons fit one row at 390px; three has never been drawn, because no modal has yet wanted an aside and a deny.

The pair now lands as written — No? beside Okay?. It used to land as NO? and OKAY?, because .btn--primary was text-transform: uppercase site-wide and shouted a line reaching for deadpan; the map carried that as an open copy complaint from #90 until #107 took the uppercase off every button at once, which was always the right blast radius for it.

The answers sit on the box's bottom border (#107) — end-aligned, side by side, snug, half inside and half out, with their hard shadows falling onto whatever is behind the box. Three treatments were drawn at phone width first: flush inside the edge read as forgotten padding, and hanging fully below lost the connection to the box. They used to stack, with the confirm spanning a row of its own, and that was a deliberate guard — with equal widths the hierarchy rested on fill colour alone, on the one control people press without reading. Snug reopens it on purpose: nothing on this site is full width by default, so fill and the deeper shadow carry the hierarchy and the words carry the rest.

Below is the two-answer shape, from askModal(). No page ships one yet — the first will be the delete-team confirm (#87) — and it is demoed here before that, so No? reaches a page as a button somebody has looked at. A component demoed only in the shape that already worked is how <a class="btn"> rendered as a raw blue link site-wide.

Nothing to do — I'm bored

The dashboard's escape hatch (#95), and the first real consumer of the two words: both of its answers close the box and do nothing else. No points, no record, no route. The site suggests something and has no follow-through — that is the joke, and it is why the deny finally had something to hang on it (denyAttrs on modalActions() exists for this caller).

It is a slot machine. The sample changes per press, not per page load, and never repeats the one already on screen — press twice and get the same word and it reads as broken rather than dry. The button below is the real one and it really works here: app.js wires it, not kit.js, because nothing about it needs a team or a database. The eighteen words live in content/chrome.js where the copy pass can find them.

It is the first modal on the site rendered shut — the hint box arrives open because what it announces has already happened; this one announces nothing until asked. And the button ships hidden, revealed by app.js: a resampling box is JavaScript by definition, and rather than hand a blocked phone a control that does nothing (#14 — nothing load-bearing), it is handed no control. Block scripts and this slot is empty, exactly as a dashboard's would be. The box has no body text, only the suggestion in the title face: a sentence underneath would be the site explaining its own joke.

14. Photos — the camera and the shots

Two of the ten tiles are photo tiles, so this is the half of the site nobody could look at without a phone, a party and a real submission behind it. All of it is shoot(), shot() and shots() from src/render.js — the same calls a game page makes.

The camera

Not a .btn. The file input is hidden by clipping inside its own label — never display: none, which would take it off the keyboard — and the label is the target. The face is the only thing that varies: the scavenger wears its prompt on it, so tapping the thing you have to photograph is what opens the camera.

One shot

A square that taps through to the full picture. The thumbnail is the one the camera already embedded in the file's EXIF — about 7KB against a 1.2MB photo, which is what keeps a tile full of them cheap on house wifi.

The yellow one is the download tile, and it is the reason this section matters more than it looks: it fires when a phone sends a format the browser will not draw — HEIC, off an iPhone — and until now it could not be seen without owning one. It names the format and downloads rather than showing a broken <img> or opening a blank page.

The strip

What a plain photo: true game renders under its hero. Neither photo tile on today's roster takes this path — both pay per unit and compose their own stage — so this is what a photo game that declares no units gets for free.

Only the newest one carries the arrival animation; the rest of the strip stays put.

The picture is public/img/kit-shot.jpg, the only image this page commits: a strip needs something in it, and /uploads/ is player data that does not exist on a fresh checkout. It is 160×120 and ~7KB — the size and shape of a real EXIF thumbnail — and it is a drawing, because this repository is public and nothing in the house belongs in it.

Both photo tiles arrange these into a list rather than a strip — see §15.

The wall

Every photograph of the night, by everyone, at /shots (#80). Three across on a phone: at two it stops reading as a pile, at four a face is 5mm across. The cells are square and object-fit: cover crops to it, so a grid of portraits and landscapes is still a grid.

The same shot() as above, with its download tile switched off, and that is this block's whole reason for existing rather than a wider .shots. Every other caller links straight at bytes, where download is exactly right. A wall cell links at the fullscreen viewer — so left alone, the yellow tile would have handed the phone a page of HTML as a file. A component demoed only in its working shape is how <a class="btn"> went out broken site-wide, so the tile is in this grid on purpose.

The filter bar

Two selects above the wall: whose camera, and which prompt. A plain GET form — pick, press, and the URL says what you are looking at. A row each, because the second one holds prompts and a prompt is a sentence; side by side they clip mid-word, and a native select cannot wrap.

Since #104 the two are snug and no longer the same width as each other, which is a select sizing to its own widest option rather than to the page. who took it is as wide as the longest team name; what it answers is at the 20rem cap and ellipsises, because a prompt is longer than that. This is the page that made a snug select dangerous in the first place — its first shot came out 454px wide on a 390px phone.

The fullscreen viewer is not in this kit and cannot be. It is a whole document rather than a component — its own <head>, no marquee, no scorebar, no menu bar, a black field and a scroll-snap track you swipe with your thumb. There is nothing to inject it into. Look at it with node scripts/walk.js shots, which shoots it with a real photograph and a real quote under it.

15. Unit rows — the list a tile pays by

Four of the ten tiles pay per unit rather than per answer, and all four put the same list on the page: the photo scavenger's ten prompts, Portrait of a stranger's gallery, Guess Who's dealt cards and Herd Mentality's five predictions. One unitRow() renders every one of them.

These classes said .prompt until #51. They are .unit now, because a prompt is one label of one unit — and three of those four pages have no prompt on them at all, just a card, a question, or a photograph with something somebody said.

Plain — one control that brought its own frame

The camera's dashed target and a field() are already boxes, so a row around them would be a box around a box. .unit carries no rules of its own: it is a hook, not a look.

Boxed — several things held together

A row takes the border, the shadow and the paper exactly when it has a label or a thumbnail to hold something against. That is derived rather than declared, so there is no flag to get wrong.

The retake is deliberately quiet — allowed and free, but never the thing being asked for, so it must not compete with the rows still open.

What is not here is the <form>. Every one of these rows sits inside one on a real tile — one per row on the scavenger, one around the whole list on Guess Who and Herd — and render.js renders no form action anywhere, because which route a control posts to is the page's business and not the design system's. That is the seam #51 settled, and the scavenger's missing send button is where you can see it.

16. The signature card — Sign Here's 3×3

One tile, open for every team from the first minute of the night, and the only grid on the site. card() and square() in src/render.js — the same calls the tile makes.

These classes said .cell until #60. They are .square now, because that is the word the glossary uses and the word the tile says out loud: write it in the square, three in a row. And .card itself was being worn by two unrelated things — this grid and the admin gallery's submission boxes — which is a collision rather than a synonym, and the cascade was quietly merging them.

Empty — how every team meets it

Nine traits, no names. The signature line is a non-breaking space rather than nothing, so the card does not jolt upward as squares fill — on a tile reopened twenty times a night that is the difference between a card and a flicker.

The nine traits above are the kit's, not the game's — the real ones live in content/games/bingo.js and are meant to be overwritten (#21). These are deliberately wider and narrower than any of them, so the box is proved against worse than it will get.

Part-signed — the state it spends the night in

Green is a square somebody signed. Signed derives itself from whether there is a name in it, so there is no flag to get wrong; the longest handle in the pool is CROISSANT, and it wraps rather than widening the column.

A line — and this is the one nobody can see without eight signatures

Yellow, with the heavier shadow, on every square of the line — a card holding two lines shows both rather than picking one. It is passed in rather than derived, because whether a square sits on a line is a fact about the card's geometry that only the scorer computes.

Yellow means finished, not correct — a line pays the whole tile instead of its squares, never on top of them (#8, #21), and the colour change is what says so. There is no wrong answer on a card: there is a line, or there is not yet.

Unlike §14 and §15, nothing is missing from this section. The grid takes no <form> — the one dropdown and the one name box sit underneath it and belong to the page — so an empty card, a part-signed one and a lit line are the entire component. That is what turned #60 into an extraction rather than another question about where the seam goes.

18. The league — the one board a guest ever sees

Every team, best first, at /league. Nothing else on this site shows one team another team's number, and for five hours nothing has: the score header in §5 is deliberately vague on purpose, and this is what it was being vague about.

The board is league() in src/render.js — the same call the page makes. The marker names only which row is yours; the teams and the scores are invented here because a demo has to have some, and they are the one thing on this page that is a drawing rather than a reading.

Why your row is a different component

The expanded row is the whole design. A guest opens this once, at one in the morning, to a column of about twelve strangers' numbers — and the first thing they want is not first place, it is themselves. Reading twelve names to find it is the wrong five seconds, so their row breaks the rhythm instead: --edge-3, the harder shadow, the tile gradient and roughly double the type, legible at arm's length while the rest of the column is not.

It is decoration over a fact, never instead of one. The row prints its rank and its score in the same two slots as every other row, and .league__flag says that's you in words — so the board reads identically with none of the styling.

First place is quieter than you are, which looks backwards and is not: it gets the yellow and the shout face and stops there. A winner loud enough to out-shout the row a reader is hunting for would invert the page's one job. A team who is both wears --you, which lands later in the cascade and wins.

A host sees no expanded row at all, and the page passes an empty youId to make that so rather than trusting it to follow from “a host is never a team” (#76). That rule is about people; the page is handed a cookie jar, and the walker holds both cookies at once. It is the right board on its own terms anyway: the pop is a reveal device for someone arriving once, and this surface refreshes every thirty seconds for five hours.

Not a .board

.board is the dense mono table two sections' worth of CSS above this one, and it says in its own comment that teams never see one. Two more reasons beyond that rule: a row that has to expand cannot live in a table whose entire design is uniform density, and .board is the exact component that had to grow contain: inline-size after it silently widened the layout viewport to 672px on a 390px phone. Nothing here is nowrap and no column is fixed, so a long team name wraps to a second line and the page stays 390 wide.

Ties share a place

1, 2, 2, 4. standings() orders equal scores on arrival time, which is fine for the sequence rows are printed in and not fine for the numeral beside them — src/scoring.js already argues exactly this for the bands. Two teams on the same score both wear the same place, and the next row skips.

19. The door — one question at a time

Onboarding is a wizard (#97). Eight to ten screens depending on how many of you there are, each one a title, at most a couple of fields, and a way on. It wears the alert box's look and is not one: no scrim, nothing fixed, nothing to dismiss — which is why its buttons are allowed their own words where a real modal's two answers are only ever No? and Okay? The two shapes it ships in

Screen one has no back and no alternative press; every screen after it has a back, and several have an alternative. Both are drawn here on purpose — a component demoed only in its loaded shape is how <a class="btn"> shipped as a raw blue link across the whole site (#66).

Type into either field above and the forward button changes its word. That is the real mechanism, not a picture of it — and it is drawn with two watched fields on purpose (#117). The captain screen carries two, because a team is one, two or three people and how many names arrive is the only thing that says which. With a single field a selector asking is some watched field empty behaves identically to one asking are they all empty; with two it does not, and the button told a captain who had just typed their mate's name that it would take them on alone. So one field here would be a demo of the one arrangement in which that cannot happen.

The counter is honest, not equal. A team walks one screen per member — a solo captain eight, a pair nine, a trio ten — and the counter says so rather than showing everyone a fixed N. Before the other names are settled it guesses two: no longer because two is the rule, but because it is the likeliest of the three and the middle one, which caps the guess at a single screen either way. From the dealt-name screen on it stops guessing and counts.

Back is a GET submission, not a link. Every screen is one form; back carries formmethod="get" and formnovalidate and re-submits the screen you are looking at to the one behind it, so what you typed travels with you in the query string and is still there coming forward. Same mechanism as the reroll and the ladder's skip. No client JS, and no server-side draft of a team that does not exist yet.

The forward word changes every screen — noted, carry on, now what, one more, good enough, that's that, Okay? — and one more only appears when another member's screen actually follows. They land as written — the primary stopped shouting in #107 (§9).

20. The prose sheet — when a paragraph gets paper

The site had three answers to this and no rule (#105). The door's paper, a game page's tile-gradient blurb, and — under the longest read in the house — nothing at all. Two or more paragraphs the page exists to have read sit on paper. The passage decides, not the page it is on.

Both demos below sit on a patch of the real party gradient, because every section on this page is white paper and a sheet of white paper on white paper proves nothing. That is the shape of check /kit has been caught by before (#102): a component shown only where it works.

The same words with no sheet

Which is the state this exists to end.
